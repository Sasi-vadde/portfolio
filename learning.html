Mixed convection in an electrolyser end-plate cooling channel

An alkaline electrolyser end plate is cooled by a serpentine channel milled into its face. Sizing that circuit needs one number: the convective heat transfer coefficient between the wall and a 30 wt% KOH stream. There is no textbook correlation for it, because the flow sits in a regime where forced convection alone does not set the answer.

01The configuration

A rectangular channel, 20 × 10 mm (Dh = 13.33 mm), swept along a serpentine centreline of 4.23 m: six straight passes joined by five 180° bends. Working fluid is 30 wt% KOH entering at 343.15 K and flowing at 110 ml/min, giving a mean axial velocity of 9.2 mm/s. The walls are held isothermal at 340.00 K, 3.15 K below the inlet.

That last choice is deliberate. Stripping out the conjugate solid, the electrochemical source term and any two-phase behaviour leaves a clean constant-wall-temperature problem, so the Nusselt number reflects convection alone. Fluid properties come from the literature rather than defaults: density and thermal expansion from Gilliam et al., heat capacity from Roux et al., viscosity benchmarked against Habibi and Li.

02The flow regime

At Re = 136 the flow is laminar by a wide margin, and at Pr = 5.83 momentum diffuses roughly six times faster than heat. Read those two alone and this looks like a Graetz problem with a known answer.

The number that changes the picture is the Richardson number, Ri = Gr/Re² = 2.87, with the Grashof number Gr built on the inlet-to-wall difference ΔT = 3.15 K. With a cold wall, a slow viscous stream and a 13 mm length scale, the density-driven cross-plane circulation has a velocity scale comparable to the axial one. At Ri above 1, buoyancy counts as a leading-order transport mechanism alongside forced convection. The thermal entrance (Graetz) effect also raises Nu over the first pass, so the scaling alone cannot split the enhancement between the two (see block 06).

03Numerical model

Steady incompressible Navier–Stokes under the Boussinesq approximation (buoyantBoussinesqSimpleFoam, OpenFOAM v2606): constant density except in the body-force term, β = 5.84×10−4 K−1, valid at ΔT = 3 K. I tried the fully compressible buoyant solver first. It is ill-conditioned here, because the hydrostatic pressure scale swamps the dynamic one.

The mesh is fully structured hexahedral, generated by a parametric script that sweeps the constant cross-section along the centreline: 48 × 32 cells per cross-section, graded to all four walls with a 216 µm first cell, 395,520 cells total. Maximum non-orthogonality 3.9°, maximum skewness 0.31. The mesh is near-orthogonal everywhere, which matters when the quantity of interest is a wall temperature gradient. Convection is second-order (linearUpwind), Laplacians corrected, and the run converged to a temperature residual of 2.5×10−7.

04Why the obvious method fails

The textbook route to h is a global energy balance: take ṁcp(Tout − Tin) over the wetted area with an LMTD driving difference. It collapses here, for a physical reason.

With NTU = hPL/ṁcp ≈ 15, the fluid equilibrates with the wall long before it reaches the outlet: the converged outlet bulk temperature is 340.000 K, equal to the wall temperature to written precision. The heat duty saturates at ṁcp(Tin − Tw) and stops depending on h, while the outlet difference Tout − Tw falls below solver precision. The LMTD then divides by a number the solution cannot resolve, and h comes out as noise.

The usable signal therefore lives only in the thermal entrance region. Its length is 0.05 Re Pr Dh ≈ 0.53 m, close to one 600 mm straight pass. That pass is the whole measurable window in a 4.23 m channel, and seeing this reshaped how I set up the extraction.

05Two extractions that could have disagreed

(a) Local wall gradient. At five stations through the first pass, Tb is the streamwise-velocity-weighted cross-sectional mean and q″ is k|∂T/∂n| integrated along the wetted perimeter (trapezoidal, perimeter-weighted, using only points lying exactly on the wall). A fixed distance tolerance in place of an exact wall test is mesh-dependent under grading and produces an artificial grid convergence that looks convincing. That bug cost me a full study before I found it.

(b) Axial decay. For constant h, θ = (Tb − Tw)/(Tin − Tw) must decay exponentially in x. Fitting ln θ against x gives a slope of −3.512 m−1 at R² = 0.9995, so h = ṁcp(−d ln θ/dx)/P = 433 W/m²K, i.e. Nu = 9.18.

The two routes share no post-processing path (one is a local differential quantity, the other an integral one), and their means agree to 6%. They also depend on different inputs, which gives that agreement extra weight (see the closure check opposite). Station by station they agree less well, as the note under the table explains.

06Where the enhancement comes from

Fully developed laminar flow in a 2:1 rectangular duct with isothermal walls gives NuT = 3.391. The simulation returns 2.5–3.2 times that value. Two effects contribute: the thermal entrance region (the Graetz problem), where Nu stays above its developed value over roughly the first pass, and the buoyant secondary flow.

At x = 66 mm the peak secondary velocity (the component normal to the channel axis) reaches 31% of the bulk axial velocity. By x = 531 mm it has decayed to 10%, and the local wall-gradient Nusselt number falls alongside it, 10.78 → 8.53. That pattern fits transverse advection sweeping near-wall fluid into the core, but the entrance effect decays over the same distance, so this run cannot separate the two. A g = 0 control run is the next check: it isolates the entrance contribution, and the difference from this case gives the buoyant share.

Closure check on the property set

Route (a) reduces to Nu = Dh|∂T/∂n|/ΔT, in which the thermal conductivity cancels, so it measures the shape of the temperature field alone. Route (b) carries ρcp/k explicitly.

Their agreement on the mean is therefore also a test of the properties. Separately, k/ρcp assembled from three literature sources matches the solver's own thermal diffusivity ν/Pr to 1.3%. An earlier version of this case carried a ν–Pr inconsistency worth 44% excess thermal diffusivity, which invalidated a whole convergence study before I caught it.

Decisions worth recording

The supplied CAD was solid-plate geometry rather than the fluid volume, with 60 non-manifold edges from a stray body. Instead of repairing it, I rebuilt the channel as a parametric swept hex mesh, which also turned the geometry into a set of variables.

I found and fixed three post-processing bugs before trusting any Nusselt number: sampling planes cutting every pass of the serpentine at once, the mesh-dependent wall tolerance above, and stale postProcessing data mixing results across runs.

Open questions I would ask myself next

Grid convergence

One mesh so far. The quality metrics are good, but they do not replace a refinement study. A three-level GCI with a computed observed order comes next; until then the Nusselt number has no error bar.

Bend physics

De = Re√(Dh/2R) ≈ 56 at the 180° turns. Curvature drives a Dean secondary flow at any De > 0, and at this value it is likely large enough to affect mixing after each bend; I have not checked it against the instability threshold for extra vortex pairs in a 2:1 duct. Every station reported here sits in the first straight pass, so the results leave bend-enhanced mixing out.

Gravity orientation

g is imposed in-plane, across the 20 mm channel width, which assumes the plate hangs vertically. A horizontal installation turns a side-heated problem into a stably or unstably stratified one and changes the buoyant contribution qualitatively.

The reference value

An external Nu ≈ 6 exists for this duty and is not yet reconciled. The leading hypothesis is an averaging definition that includes the thermally saturated downstream region, with the same physics underneath. I have not tested it yet.

Representative vs. measured geometry

The current layout is a representative serpentine (6 × 600 mm passes, 80 mm pitch). Substituting the measured plate circuit shortens the passes without moving the regime. The 0.53 m entrance window then covers a different share of each pass, so the sampling stations move too.

Modelling scope

Constant properties, Boussinesq, single phase, no conjugate conduction in the nickel plate. Each is defensible at ΔT = 3 K. Each has a cost, and I would relax the conjugate coupling first.
